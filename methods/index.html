How the numbers are made

Plain-language explanations of the adjustments behind the site, with the real figures.

The arena adjustment

Hits, giveaways, takeaways and blocked shots are counted by people, and some buildings count more generously than others.

What it is. Every NHL arena has its own off-ice scorers. A bump along the boards might be a hit in one building and nothing in another. Left alone, that makes teams look more or less physical just because of where they play half their games.

How it’s calculated. For each arena, we take every game played there over three seasons (2023-24 to 2025-26) and work out how often each event is recorded per 60 minutes of play, for both teams combined. Then we do the same for that home team’s road games. Dividing the first by the second gives the arena’s factor. Because even three seasons is a limited sample, each factor is pulled a little of the way back toward 1.00.

A worked example. At Scotiabank Arena, scorers recorded 52.29 hits per 60. In TOR’s road games, the same team and its opponents were credited with 42.75. 52.29 ÷ 42.75 = 1.22, which settles at 1.19 after the pull toward 1.00. So a night with 30 recorded hits there counts as 25.2.

How it’s shown. We never display an adjusted count, because that would look like a number of hits that nobody recorded. Instead, arena-adjusted stats appear as a score, where 100 is league average. The “Hits and takeaways” switch at the top of every page flips to the raw numbers exactly as recorded.

Home and road

Separately from any one building, home teams are credited with more of these events everywhere in the league. Over three seasons, home teams were given 21.69 hits per 60 to visitors’ 20.89, 5.66 takeaways to 5.17, and also more giveaways: 13.02 to 12.08.

That could be scorers watching the home side more closely, or teams genuinely having the puck more at home; public data can’t separate the two. Either way, it would flatter a team in the middle of a long homestand. So events are weighted to put home and road games on the same footing, the same way shots are.

We also tested whether particular buildings favour their home team more than others do. The differences were there in any single season but did not repeat from one season to the next, which means they are noise rather than a habit. Correcting for them would make the numbers worse, so we don’t.

Blending this season with last

Early in a season, a handful of games says little about some traits and a lot about others.

With “This season and last” selected, each number is a mix of this season’s games and the team’s previous season. How much it leans on last season depends on the trait. We measured, from three past seasons, how many games it takes before a trait is as much signal as noise:

  • depth4 games
  • Shot volume4 games
  • Forecheck pressure5 games
  • Physicality5 games
  • Shot suppression9 games
  • Shot quality11 games
  • Point-shot reliance11 games
  • Pace16 games
  • Quality allowed18 games
  • Power play22 games
  • Penalty kill41 games
  • Power kill43 games
  • Discipline58 games
  • Off-turnover offense101 games
  • Breakdowns avoided139 games
  • Goaltending145 games
  • Second chancesover 300 games

So shot volume is trustworthy within a couple of weeks, while goaltending and penalty killing stay close to league average until much later. “This season only” turns the blending off and shows the raw swing.

Score and venue adjustment

Teams that are behind shoot more, and so do home teams. Shot-based numbers are corrected for both.

A team protecting a lead sits back; a team chasing one throws everything at the net. Comparing raw shot totals would reward teams for trailing. Each 5-on-5 shot is therefore weighted by the score at the time and by whether the shooter is at home, so that across the league the home and away totals balance in every score situation.

How dangerous a chance is

Every shot gets a rating: how often shots like it go in.

The site uses its own expected-goals model, trained on about 360,000 shots from three seasons. For each shot it looks at where it was taken from, what kind of shot it was, what happened just before (a save, a turnover, a faceoff) and how long ago, and the manpower on the ice. It deliberately doesn’t know who shot or who was in net.

Tested on a full season it had never seen, a rating of 10% meant the puck went in 10.0% of the time. Because one model rates every season, numbers from different years can be compared fairly. See how it rated the chances in recent games.

Numbering the lines

First line, second line and so on are decided by ice time, not reputation.

A team’s lines and defense pairs are numbered by how much 5-on-5 ice time each group has played together over its last ten games. The group with the most is the first line. That means a line can be numbered lower than fans expect if its players have been shuffled between different linemates.

Each line’s ratings compare it with the same-numbered line on the other 31 teams: first lines with first lines, third pairs with third pairs.

Player ratings

Each player is compared with others at his position: forwards with forwards, defensemen with defensemen.

Every rating is a rate: shot attempts, expected goals, assists, hits and so on per 60 minutes, mostly at 5-on-5. A player who has played a few games is pulled toward the average for his position, and how hard depends on how quickly that rating settles. Shot volume and hitting settle within a few games; finishing and power-play scoring take seasons, so they barely move early on. We measured this by splitting three past seasons into odd and even games and checking how well one half predicts the other.

“This season and last” also leans on the player’s own previous season. The likely range is the middle 80% of results when his games are reshuffled at random. A player is only listed as better or worse than most when even the cautious end of that range is clearly away from average. Percentiles compare him with regulars at his position: at least 20 games over the two seasons, or 3 this season.

Forwards are compared with all forwards, except where centers and wingers really play different games: shooting volume, creating their own chances, shot blocking, hardest shot and distance skated. There, centers are compared with centers and wingers with wingers. We checked every rating over two seasons of regulars and split only where the gap between the two groups was large (wingers shoot more and harder; centers block more shots and skate farther). Defense showed no gap at all.

“Creating chances” and “Preventing chances” isolate what a player adds at 5-on-5. Every shift of this season and last goes into one regression that rates all players at once, so each player gets credit or blame only for what is left after his linemates, the opponents on the ice, where the shift started and the score are accounted for. A shutdown center who faces top lines every night is not punished for their chances, and a winger riding with a star does not take the star’s credit. This is regularized adjusted plus-minus (RAPM), the method public analysts such as Evolving-Hockey use; the “regularized” part pulls every player toward average, harder the less he has played, with the strength chosen by testing predictions on games the model had not seen. It measures expected goals, so it sees chances, not things public data does not record, such as good stick work that stops a pass.

Players who changed teams: last season still counts, but not equally for everything. Over the last three seasons, habits that travel with a player (shooting volume, the danger of his shots, hitting, shot blocking, drawing and taking penalties) carried over to a new team almost as well as for players who stayed put. Setting up goals, finishing, faceoffs and power-play chances carried over far less, because they depend on linemates and role, so for a player on a new team those four lean on last season only 35% to 60% as much as usual.

Special teams: power-play units are built from the current season only, and the Trigger, Distributor and Net-front tags count only a player’s games with his current team. Penalty kills are too loose for fixed units (the most common foursome covers only about a tenth of a typical team’s shorthanded time), so each team’s penalty killers are grouped by when they go on: the ones who start most kills (usually for the defensive-zone faceoff), the second wave who come on after the first change, and spot duty. Most teams work this way. “Shorthanded threat” is the chances a team creates while killing penalties with him on the ice; it holds up from season to season, so it is a real skill.

“Competition” on a player’s Role section is how dangerous his 5-on-5 opponents are, using those same ratings: the five opposing skaters’ offense added up, averaged over his ice time, split by home and road because the home coach has the last change.

Skating speed, bursts, shot speed, distance and offensive-zone time come from NHL EDGE, the league’s own puck and player tracking. They are measurements rather than estimates, so they carry no likely range, and we rank them against the same position groups ourselves.

What “per 60” means

Most rates on the site are per 60 minutes of ice time.

A top line plays far more than a fourth line, and a team with a lot of power plays gets far more power-play shots than one with few. To compare them fairly, almost every rate on the site is scaled to 60 minutes of ice time in that situation: “2.9 expected goals per 60” means what the group would produce in a full hour at its current pace. Ice time itself is written as minutes and seconds, like 9:15.

Blind spots

  • Passes before a shot aren’t in public data, so expected-goal numbers can’t see a cross-ice feed. Goalies behind teams that allow those look worse than they are.
  • Rush chances can’t be picked out from the league’s public game data, which records too little between the blue lines. Shots flagged by the usual timing rule (within four seconds of an event outside the attacking zone) actually score less often than ordinary shots, so the site shows no rush numbers. Real rush counts come from people or cameras tracking zone entries.
  • Where in the net a goal or save went (glove side, blocker, five-hole) isn’t recorded in any public NHL data. Missed shots do say how they missed (wide left, wide right, over the net, off the post), and player pages show that.
  • Defender positions aren’t tracked publicly, so “breakdowns” are inferred from dangerous chances within five seconds of a team losing the puck.
